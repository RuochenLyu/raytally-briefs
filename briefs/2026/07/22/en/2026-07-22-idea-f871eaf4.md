## Idea

One Step When Your Mind Is Full
When too many thoughts arrive at once and leave you frozen, speak them aloud and receive just one tangible action you can finish within two minutes.

## Product concept

When someone is pacing around a room with emails to answer, laundry to do, bills to worry about, and a meeting to prepare for all crowding their mind, they can hold down their phone and talk for a minute. The product does not ask them to categorize anything first or push them to turn every thought into a task. It simply holds their words, including thoughts they have not fully worked out yet. When they finish, the screen shows only one tangible action that can be completed within two minutes. It must come from what the user just said, such as putting a cup in the sink, putting a pillbox in a bag, or opening the file that needs a reply. Once the user marks it complete, they see the next item. Things they cannot act on yet remain under “Decide Later” and “Just a Worry,” so they do not compete for attention now. After a few steps, the product separates what the user has started from what still needs scheduling, and they can move an item into a calendar or list. The first version does not diagnose anxiety, attention issues, or other health conditions, and it does not plan the user’s entire day. It only helps people restart with one visible small action in the few minutes when their thoughts are blocking action.

## Direction (model inference, not independently verified)

Target user: Adults who work alone or manage daily life on their own. When everything arrives at once, they pace around a room or repeatedly switch between apps. They do not lack things to do; every task is competing to come first. Common moments include before a meeting, before leaving home, while wrapping up the evening, or when housework has piled up. They need a starting action that requires no further comparison, not a complete plan.

Minimal entry point: Start with a hold-to-talk iPhone flow. Use Apple Speech to receive microphone audio and return a transcription. The backend extracts only the objects, places, actions, and constraints in the user’s own words. Candidate actions must be ready to start immediately and expected to take no more than two minutes. Rank them first by whether they can be done in the current setting, then by whether they require waiting for someone else. Return one action at a time, along with the original sentence so the user can verify it. Sort everything else only into “Decide Later” and “Just a Worry.” The first release does not create a day plan or automatically write to external tools. After the user completes a few steps, ask for confirmation before exporting an item to a calendar or list.

The strongest case against: The central risk is choosing the wrong first action. The model may miss the location, timing, or state of an object and suggest something that cannot be done in the moment. If users must repeatedly edit its suggestion, getting started will cost more than opening a notes app. Voice input may contain billing, health, and work information, so storage and deletion rules must be clear. Mistakenly classifying a worry as an action item will make users feel unheard; putting too much under “Just a Worry” can also hide important tasks. The product must avoid implying therapeutic effects, or trust and compliance costs will rise.

## Competitors & gaps (model inference)

- Todoist Ramble: Todoist Ramble already captures spoken thoughts in natural language and turns them into tasks with projects, dates, due dates, and priorities. It works well for quickly clearing what is on someone’s mind and feeds into an established task-management workflow. Its goal, however, is still to place each item accurately in a task system. When someone is stuck, a full list can create more pressure to choose. The opening here is not better task recognition, but withholding the rest of the list. The product first selects one visible, tangible action that requires no waiting. Only after it is done does the next step appear, making initiation itself the core flow. The trade-off is that this restraint sacrifices overview and bulk-editing efficiency. It is better suited to brief stalls than everyday project management.
- Goblin Tools Magic ToDo: Goblin Tools’ Magic ToDo can automatically break a task into steps and adjust the level of detail according to how difficult the user finds it. It already covers task breakdown, categorization, time estimates, and several export options. It is useful when a user faces one clear but oversized task. The input here is more disordered: it may combine worries, errands, and half-formed thoughts. The product cannot expand every sentence into subtasks, or the list will quickly balloon. The real opening is to first distinguish what can be acted on immediately from what should be decided later and what is simply a worry. The interface must also reveal only one tangible action at a time. This reduces choice, but makes the product more dependent on the first action fitting the situation. If that first step is wrong, Goblin Tools’ browsable list may be easier to correct.
